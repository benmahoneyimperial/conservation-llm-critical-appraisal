Domain 1 - Risk of Confounding Biases Checklist (Structured for LLM Use)

Question ID: 1.1
Category: General (please answer)
When to answer: Always
Question: Is it possible for the impact of the exposure or the effectiveness of the intervention to be confounded in this study?
Notes/Examples: Select Yes/Seemingly yes when randomisation is considered to deconfound. Confounding occurs when a variable influences both the intervention/exposure and the outcome.
Allowed answers: Yes | Seemingly yes | Seemingly no | No

Question ID: 1.2
Category: Conditional
When to answer: If 1.1 is Yes or Seemingly yes; otherwise select Not applicable
Question: Did the author(s) control for all the potential confounders?
Notes/Examples: All potential confounders should have been identified (e.g., through a causal model) and controlled for. Select No if any potential confounder was not controlled for.
Allowed answers: Yes | Seemingly yes | Seemingly no | No | Unclear (No) | Not applicable

Question ID: 1.3
Category: Conditional
When to answer: If 1.2 is No, Seemingly no, or Unclear; otherwise select Not applicable
Question: Is there any justifiable reason for not controlling for all the potential confounders (so that omission of some potential confounders is unlikely to influence the assessment of the effectiveness or impact)?
Notes/Examples: Select Yes/Seemingly yes when there is evidence that omission of some confounders does not affect the assessment. This may be the case if adjusting all confounders would lead to overadjustment, or if an instrumental variable is used. An instrumental variable is not associated with the confounder(s), is associated with the intervention/exposure, but does not directly influence the outcome (see Hernán & Robins 2020).
Allowed answers: Yes | Seemingly yes | Seemingly no | No | Not applicable

Question ID: 1.4
Category: Conditional
When to answer: If 1.2 is Yes/Seemingly yes, or 1.3 is Yes/Seemingly yes; otherwise select Not applicable
Question: Were the potential confounders that were controlled for (and/or the instrumental variable used, if applicable) likely to be measured accurately and precisely enough?
Notes/Examples: Measurements of factors may be nominal (categorical), ordinal (ranks), or scale. Consider whether measurement error in confounders could affect the validity of adjustment.
Allowed answers: Yes | Seemingly yes | Seemingly no | No | Unclear (No) | Not applicable

Question ID: 1.5
Category: Conditional
When to answer: If 1.4 was answered; otherwise select Not applicable
Question: Did the author(s) analyse the effect appropriately by taking into account the potential confounders, as well as the issue of accuracy and precision of the measurements of the potential confounders (and the instrumental variable if applicable)?
Notes/Examples: Examples of appropriate adjustment techniques include stratification, matching, inverse probability weighting, standardisation, G-estimation, and instrumental variable estimation.
Allowed answers: Yes | Seemingly yes | Seemingly no | No | Unclear (No) | Not applicable

Question ID: 1.6
Category: Optional
When to answer: Optional; assessors may skip if unfeasible
Question: What are the predicted magnitude and direction of biases due to confounding?
Notes/Examples: Quantitative assessment (e.g., through simulation) may be conducted to predict the magnitude and direction of bias for this study result.
Allowed answers: Intervention or exposure intolerably favored (*) | Intervention or exposure tolerably favored (**) | Comparator intolerably favored (*) | Comparator tolerably favored (**) | Intolerably towards no effect (*) | Tolerably towards no effect (**) | Intolerably away from no effect (*) | Tolerably away from no effect (**) | Unpredictable | Skip

Source note: Table B1 and (c) 2021 the authors.
Bias magnitude definitions: * Intolerable means the study result should not be considered valid enough in relation to predicted bias magnitude. ** Tolerable means the study result could be considered valid enough in relation to predicted bias magnitude.
