Domain 1 - Confounding Bias Checklist (Structured for LLM Use)

Question ID: 1.1
Category: General (please answer)
When to answer: Always
Question: Is it possible for the impact of the exposure or the effectiveness of the intervention to be confounded in this study?
Notes/Examples: E.g., select Yes or Seemingly yes when randomization is considered to deconfound.
Allowed answers: Yes | Seemingly yes | Seemingly no | No

Question ID: 1.2
Category: Conditional
When to answer: If 1.1 is Yes or Seemingly yes; otherwise select Not applicable
Question: Did the author(s) control for all the potential confounders?
Notes/Examples: All potential confounders should be listed in Appendix A.
Allowed answers: Yes | Seemingly yes | Seemingly no | No | Unclear (No) | Not applicable

Question ID: 1.3
Category: Conditional
When to answer: If 1.2 is No, Seemingly no, or Unclear; otherwise select Not applicable
Question: Is there any justifiable reason for not controlling for all the potential confounders (so that omission of some potential confounders is unlikely to influence the assessment of effectiveness or impact)?
Notes/Examples: E.g., select Yes or Seemingly yes when there is evidence that omission of some potential confounders does not affect the assessment of effectiveness or impact. This may be the case if adjusting all potential confounders will lead to overadjustment, or an instrumental variable is used for estimating effectiveness or impact. Instrumental variable is a variable that (1) is not associated with the confounder(s), (2) is associated with the intervention/exposure, but (3) does not directly influence the outcome. If used appropriately, it enables valid estimation. See Hernan and Robins 2020 for guidance.
Allowed answers: Yes | Seemingly yes | Seemingly no | No | Not applicable

Question ID: 1.4
Category: Conditional
When to answer: If 1.2 or 1.3 is Yes or Seemingly yes; otherwise select Not applicable
Question: Were the potential confounders that were controlled for (and/or the instrumental variable used, if applicable) likely to be measured accurately and precisely enough?
Notes/Examples: Measurements of factors may be nominal (categorical), ordinal (ranks), or scale.
Allowed answers: Yes | Seemingly yes | Seemingly no | No | Unclear (No) | Not applicable

Question ID: 1.5
Category: Conditional
When to answer: If you have answered 1.4; otherwise select Not applicable
Question: Did the author(s) analyze the effect appropriately by taking into account potential confounders, as well as the issue of accuracy and precision of measurements of potential confounders (and the instrumental variable, if applicable)?
Notes/Examples: Appropriate adjustment techniques for confounding may include stratification, matching, inverse probability weighting, standardization, G-estimation, and instrumental variable estimation.
Allowed answers: Yes | Seemingly yes | Seemingly no | No | Unclear (No) | Not applicable

Question ID: 1.6
Category: Optional
When to answer: Optional; assessors may skip if unfeasible
Question: What are the predicted magnitude and direction of biases due to confounding?
Notes/Examples: Quantitative assessment (e.g., simulation) may be conducted by risk-of-bias assessor(s) to predict magnitude and direction of bias for this study result.
Allowed answers: Intervention or exposure intolerably favored (*) | Intervention or exposure tolerably favored (**) | Comparator intolerably favored (*) | Comparator tolerably favored (**) | Intolerably towards no effect (*) | Tolerably towards no effect (**) | Intolerably away from no effect (*) | Tolerably away from no effect (**) | Unpredictable | Skip

Source note: (c) 2021 the authors.
Bias magnitude definitions: * Intolerable means the study result should not be considered valid enough in relation to predicted bias magnitude. ** Tolerable means the study result could be considered valid enough in relation to predicted bias magnitude.
