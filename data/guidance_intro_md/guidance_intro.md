# Collaboration for Environmental Evidence Critical Appraisal Tool

## Part A: General Description of the Tool

This critical appraisal tool evaluates the risk of bias (or threats to internal validity) of primary studies assessing the effectiveness of interventions or impacts of exposures in environmental management. Application of the tool involves:

1. Answering the checklist questions within individual risk-of-bias criteria.
2. Judging the risk of bias within individual risk-of-bias criteria.
3. Making an overall judgement about risk of bias for the study findings, including estimates of effectiveness of intervention or impact of exposure.

# Understanding Risk of Bias

Bias or inaccuracy is referred to as deviation from the truth or a systematic error in a study's findings, including inference that is made in the study. Although the distinction between bias and imprecision, or random error, should be kept clearly in mind when applying this tool, biases in real-world data cannot often be quantified. Thus, perfectly distinguishing between bias and imprecision is not possible unless true values are known.

The concept of risk of bias, or measures of threats to internal validity, was developed to evaluate how susceptible studies are to biases rather than trying to quantify biases directly.

Study findings will always have some risk of bias because it is not often possible to prove that there are no biases in findings. Having a risk of bias does not mean that the findings are biased, but there is always some possibility that findings are biased to some extent. The spectrum of risk of bias is categorical:

- Low
- Medium
- High

# Assumptions of the Tool

## Subjectivity

In any assessment of risk, it inevitably requires subjective judgements to be made, and so application of this tool does not make conduct of critical appraisal 100% objective. For example, this tool asks assessors for some subjective judgements using terms such as 'acceptable', 'tolerable' and 'accurate enough', and so there is some degree of freedom for assessors how certain aspects of studies, that might be sources of bias, are judged for risk of bias. This subjectivity allows some flexibility to reflect the extent of acceptability of risk of bias based on the nature of primary research and evidence synthesis. Assessors should check consistency between them before and when formally applying the tool. Reading this document alone should not be considered as formal training of critical appraisal. Assessors may need to consult evidence synthesis and subject experts before formally applying the tool.

# Study Designs

For a primary study assessing effectiveness or impact, its design, or type of comparison made, may be referred to as one of the following:

- Control-impact (CI)
- Before-after (BA)
- Before-after control-impact (BACI)
- Randomised controlled trial (RCT)

While relatively more rigorous study designs, such as experimental BACI and RCT compared to CI or BA, may be employed to increase precision, or to control variances, and accuracy, it is theoretically possible that study findings are not biased if sources of bias are sufficiently eliminated in any study design, or approaches are justified on a case-by-case basis based on the addressed causal structure and available data.

Thus, this tool does not assume that differences in study designs alone affect the accuracy of study findings. In other words, the tool does not assume that study designs themselves are biased, but it assumes that effect estimates may be biased if sources of bias are not controlled for or not taken into account.

The tool does not require coded data on study designs for assessing risk of bias, although such coded data may be useful for sensitivity or subgroup analysis.

The term 'comparator' refers to an overarching comparison group or condition. A comparator may be:

- A control group receiving no intervention or exposure.
- A baseline measurement taken before an intervention or exposure.
- An alternative intervention or exposure.

The comparator is the group or condition against which the intervention or exposure is compared to estimate effectiveness or impact.

## Study-design classification rules

### Randomised controlled trial

A randomised controlled trial is a study in which participants, subjects, areas, or other units are explicitly allocated to intervention and comparator groups using randomisation.

- Study type: Experimental.
- Comparison structure: Before and after data may be available for both groups, but complete before-and-after data are not required for a study to be an RCT.
- Criterion 4 applies because an experimental treatment or exposure is assigned.

### Before-after control-impact

A before-after control-impact study includes before-and-after data for both an intervention or impact group and a comparator group.

- Study type: Observational or experimental.
- Classify the study as experimental if the intervention or exposure was formally assigned or applied by the researchers.
- Classify the study as observational if the researchers compared existing groups or conditions without formally assigning or applying the intervention or exposure.
- The presence of before-and-after data does not by itself establish that the study is experimental.
- Criterion 3 applies to observational BACI studies.
- Criterion 4 applies to experimental BACI studies.

### Before-after

A before-after study compares the state of an intervention or exposure group before and after the intervention or exposure.

- Study type: Observational or experimental.
- The baseline state is the comparator.
- There is no concurrent separate control group.
- Classify the study as experimental if the intervention or exposure was formally assigned or applied by the researchers.
- Classify the study as observational if the researchers observed an existing intervention or exposure without formally assigning or applying it.
- Criterion 3 applies to observational BA studies.
- Criterion 4 applies to experimental BA studies.

### Control-impact

A control-impact study compares an intervention or impact group with a separate comparator group after the intervention or exposure.

- Study type: Observational or experimental.
- There is no before-intervention or baseline data.
- The comparison relies entirely on post-intervention or post-exposure groups.
- Classify the study as experimental if the intervention or exposure was formally assigned or applied by the researchers.
- Classify the study as observational if the researchers observed existing groups or conditions without formally assigning or applying the intervention or exposure.
- Criterion 3 applies to observational CI studies.
- Criterion 4 applies to experimental CI studies.

## Experimental versus observational studies

Primary studies may be experimental or observational.

Experimental studies are studies in which interventions or exposures are formally applied or assigned by the researchers to evaluate effects.

Observational studies are studies in which researchers compare groups or conditions of interest without formally applying or assigning the interventions or exposures being evaluated.

Study type alone does not determine the risk of bias. The tool does not assume that experimental studies are inherently less biased than observational studies, or that observational studies are inherently more biased than experimental studies. Risk of bias depends on whether relevant sources of bias are controlled for or taken into account.

The study type determines whether Criterion 3 or Criterion 4 applies:

- Criterion 3 applies only to observational studies.
- Criterion 4 applies only to experimental studies.
- Exactly six of the seven criteria are assessed for each study finding.
- Criterion 3 is not applicable to experimental studies.
- Criterion 4 is not applicable to observational studies.

When the study type cannot be determined from the available information, select “Unclear” for the study-type classification and assess the applicable criteria conservatively. Do not classify a study as experimental solely because it uses the terms “treatment,” “control,” or “intervention.”

# Intervention or Exposure

This tool can be used for both intervention and exposure studies, and there is no difference in the process of risk-of-bias assessment between exposure and intervention studies. The terms intervention and exposure are used at the same time in the tool as both effectiveness and impacts are measures of effects of causes.

Interventions may be characterised based on desired or intended outcomes that are indeed measured in studies, such as an intervention to remove pollutants where removal of pollutants is measured. Exposures may be characterised based on undesired or unintended outcomes, such as exposure of soil fauna to fertilisers when fertilisers are intended to increase crop yield.

In most cases, the distinction between interventions and exposures may be made based on specific outcomes, or endpoints, that are measured in relation to causes of interest.

In environmental management, exposure studies may be conducted experimentally. The distinction between interventions and exposures may not always be obvious in the environmental sector.

# Individual Risk-of-Bias Criteria

There are diverse definitions of forms of bias, and they may be used differently by different people in different disciplines. There are also overlaps in meaning among some forms of bias. Thus, it is necessary to determine what sources of bias are investigated and when the sources of bias may occur.

This tool uses seven risk-of-bias criteria:

1. Risk of confounding biases
2. Risk of post-intervention/exposure selection biases
3. Risk of misclassified comparison biases
4. Risk of performance biases
5. Risk of detection biases
6. Risk of outcome reporting biases
7. Risk of outcome assessment biases

Criterion 3 is applicable only to observational studies. Criterion 4 is applicable only to experimental studies. Therefore, assessors answer checklist questions for six risk-of-bias criteria for each study finding.

The criteria are defined as follows:

1. **Risk of confounding biases:** Risk of biases due to an uncontrolled or inappropriately controlled variable, or confounder, that influences both the intervention or exposure and the outcome.
2. **Risk of post-intervention/exposure selection biases:** Risk of biases arising from systematic differences in the selection of subjects or areas into the study or analysis after intervention or exposure.
3. **Risk of misclassified comparison biases:** Risk of biases arising from misclassification or measurement of the intervention, exposure, or comparator. This criterion applies to observational studies only.
4. **Risk of performance biases:** Risk of biases due to an altered treatment procedure of interest. This criterion applies to experimental studies only.
5. **Risk of detection biases:** Risk of biases arising from systematic differences in the measurement of outcomes.
6. **Risk of outcome reporting biases:** Risk of biases in reporting study findings.
7. **Risk of outcome assessment biases:** Risk of biases due to errors in applied statistical methods.

The criteria are intended to define the sources of bias assessed by the tool rather than to provide universal definitions of forms of bias.

# Criterion 1: Risk of Confounding Biases

This criterion is concerned with biases due to uncontrolled or inappropriately controlled variables, or confounders, that influence both the intervention or exposure and the outcome.

A causal directed acyclic graph, also known as a causal model or causal diagram, can be a useful tool for investigating the potential for confounding. In the language of causal directed acyclic graphs, the presence of confounding is illustrated as an unblocked 'backdoor path' that affects both the intervention or exposure and the outcome.

If there is confounding, the confounder, also known as a concomitant or covariate, will distort the association between an intervention or exposure and an outcome. More technically, if the assumption of exchangeability between groups does not hold due to the presence of shared causes of the intervention or exposure and the outcome, effect estimates will be biased due to confounding.

An example of confounding is that if the effect of population density of salmonids on their survival is studied, the presence of predators may be a confounder because it may influence both survival and population density. Controlling for the presence of predators may reduce the risk of confounding bias in this instance.

When a confounder is controlled for, such as through stratification, conditional exchangeability between groups may hold. The assumption of randomness may not be violated within a stratum or strata of the confounder variable, and a valid effect estimate may be provided within a stratum or strata.

Baseline confounding is confounding caused by differences between groups that exist before the intervention or exposure. Baseline confounding may sometimes be called selection bias, but it must be assessed under Criterion 1 in this tool.

Assessors should list all potential confounders relevant to the addressed causal structure. Potential confounders should be identified using subject knowledge and a general causal model before formally assessing risk of confounding biases.

A variable should be treated as a potential confounder when it influences, or is associated with, both the intervention or exposure and the outcome. A variable that only predicts the outcome is not necessarily a confounder. A variable that changes the size or direction of the intervention or exposure effect is an effect modifier, not necessarily a confounder.

Potential confounders may be controlled by study design or statistical analysis. Examples include randomisation, restriction, matching, stratification, standardisation, regression adjustment, and other appropriate methods. A confounder should be considered adequately controlled only when the study provides sufficient information to judge that the method was appropriate for the addressed causal structure.

Do not classify baseline imbalance as post-intervention/exposure selection bias. If the imbalance existed before the intervention or exposure, assess it under Criterion 1.

# Criterion 2: Risk of Post-Intervention/Exposure Selection Biases

This criterion is concerned with biases arising from systematic differences in the selection of subjects or areas into the study or analysis after intervention or exposure.

This is a distinct phenomenon from baseline confounding, which may also be called baseline selection bias. The term 'post-intervention/exposure selection bias' is used to distinguish this source of bias from baseline confounding.

A statistical population of inference often consists of subjects or areas with different characteristics. These characteristics may be effect modifiers, meaning that the effect may vary across levels of the variables.

If subjects or areas with different characteristics are followed up or selected after intervention or exposure, and the characteristics are effect modifiers, an effect may be overestimated or underestimated depending on the addressed causal structure and the characteristics of the subjects or areas.

Post-intervention/exposure selection bias may arise from:

- Differential loss to follow-up.
- Differential attrition.
- Selective outcome sampling.
- Selective inclusion in the analysis.
- Haphazard or systematic selection of subjects or areas after intervention or exposure.
- Unequal missing data between intervention or exposure and comparator groups.

To classify a problem under Criterion 2, the relevant imbalance or selection must arise after the intervention or exposure, or during follow-up or outcome analysis. If the imbalance existed before the intervention or exposure, assess it under Criterion 1 instead.

Unequal loss of data does not automatically create bias. The risk depends on whether the pattern of loss differs between comparison groups and whether the characteristics associated with loss modify the effect or otherwise affect the comparison.

If percentages of missing data are the same, or nearly the same, and the collected data still allow a valid comparison within a stratum or strata, missing data may not create bias from the perspective of systematic differences in missing data between intervention or exposure and comparator groups.

# Criterion 3: Risk of Misclassified Comparison Biases (Observational Studies Only)

This criterion is concerned with biases arising from misclassification or measurement of the intervention, exposure, or comparator. This criterion is applicable to observational studies only.

If experimental treatments or exposures are applied, this criterion is not applicable. Experimental studies are assessed under Criterion 4 instead.

Accurate and precise definitions of intervention or exposure and comparator groups are necessary for avoiding misclassification. Intervention or exposure and comparator may be misclassified by researchers who usually classify them based on available information and data, which may not always be accurate.

For example, if the effect of pesticides on the mortality of honey bees is assessed in an observational study, it may be necessary to define which pesticides or compounds are relevant and what counts as exposure, such as compounds found in pollen, beeswax, or honey bees.

When certain individuals are classified in a 'no exposure' group as a comparator, evidence of 'no exposure' and descriptions of which compounds are screened for in which objects may be necessary.

If screened compounds are not sufficiently extensive in the 'no exposure' comparator group and researchers fail to detect pesticide residue in the 'no exposure' group when residue is present, this will lead to a misclassified comparison. The effect may be biased towards the null in this instance because the comparison is actually exposure to pesticides versus exposure to pesticides.

If researchers are interested in specific compounds only, and there is supporting information that the comparison was correctly classified, the existence of other pesticides in samples that are not the focus may be an issue of confounding. Such aspects should be assessed under Criterion 1.

Misclassification may be:

- Non-differential, when the classification error is independent of the outcome or comparison group.
- Differential, when the classification error is related to the outcome, comparison group, knowledge of group status, or other factors that differ between groups.

Non-differential misclassification may reduce the accuracy or precision of the effect estimate and may often attenuate an association, but the direction and magnitude of bias cannot be assumed in every situation.

# Criterion 4: Risk of Performance Biases (Experimental Studies Only)

This criterion is concerned with biases due to altered treatment procedures of interest or deviated initiation, implementation, or discontinuation either by persons who apply experimental treatments or by subjects who receive the treatments.

This criterion applies to experimental studies involving assigned interventions or exposures. It applies to both experimental interventions and experimentally assigned exposures.

If no experimental treatments or exposures are applied, this criterion is not applicable. Observational studies are assessed under Criterion 3 instead.

In this tool, treatments are defined as different procedures to be compared for measuring effects. The term 'control' is often used loosely to denote 'no treatment' in scientific literature. However, it is technically a treatment, meaning a procedure to be compared against. Therefore, both the intervention or exposure and the control, or alternative intervention or exposure, need to be assessed under this criterion.

Performance bias may arise if treatments are altered either intentionally or unintentionally after the procedures have taken place and if the alterations are not taken into account.

Examples include:

- Altered procedures are not reflected on the data collection sheet.
- Areas to which altered procedures were applied are not excluded.
- The planned treatment dose differs from the dose actually applied.
- Treatment implementation differs between groups.
- Co-interventions are provided unequally between groups.
- Treatment adherence, initiation, or discontinuation differs between groups.
- Treatment changes are influenced by knowledge of group assignment or interim outcomes.

Lack of blinding alone does not establish performance bias. Awareness of treatment allocation creates a risk of performance bias when it leads to altered implementation, co-interventions, adherence, discontinuation, or other differences in treatment procedures between comparison groups.

If there are no deviations from procedures of interest, or there is supporting information that no alterations occurred, and procedures were successfully implemented for all subjects or areas without any influence of awareness, awareness of the procedures alone will not affect the estimate of an effect from the perspective of altered treatment procedures.

# Criterion 5: Risk of Detection Biases

This criterion is concerned with biases arising from systematic differences in the measurement of outcomes.

Systematic errors in measurement of outcomes may occur if outcome data are determined differently between groups, either intentionally or unintentionally.

Examples include:

- Outcome assessors use different measurement procedures between groups.
- Different instruments, devices, extractants, calibration methods, or protocols are used between groups.
- Outcome assessors' knowledge of group assignment influences measurement.
- Outcome assessors' expectations, experience, or desire to obtain a particular result influences measurement.
- Subjective outcomes are assessed differently between groups.

Blinding of outcome assessors can reduce the risk of differential outcome measurement. However, lack of blinding does not automatically establish high risk of detection bias.

When outcome measurement is objective, automated, mechanically recorded, or otherwise unlikely to be influenced by knowledge of group assignment, the risk of differential detection bias may be low even if blinding is not reported.

When outcome measurement is subjective or requires human judgement, lack of assessor blinding creates a greater risk of detection bias.

The absence of a statement about blinding should generally be recorded as unclear rather than automatically treated as evidence that blinding was not performed. The risk judgement should also consider the subjectivity and susceptibility of the outcome measurement to influence.

If the same biased device or measurement procedure is used in both groups and affects both groups in the same way, the bias may cancel out and may not affect the estimated contrast between groups.

# Criterion 6: Risk of Outcome Reporting Biases

This criterion is concerned with biases in reporting study findings.

Outcome reporting biases may arise if findings are selectively disclosed in reporting that does not reflect actual findings. Selective disclosure may appear at three different levels:

- Selective disclosure of findings from multiple measurements.
- Selective disclosure of findings from multiple subgroups or subpopulations.
- Selective disclosure of findings from multiple analyses.

It is theoretically possible that findings are selectively disclosed to report only statistically significant, interesting, or desired results.

Outcome reporting bias may be suspected when:

- Authors do not report non-significant results or no or small effects.
- Results are reported for only some of the outcomes, measurements, subgroups, or analyses described in the methods.
- The reported outcomes differ from predefined hypotheses or methods.
- The study reports post hoc outcomes or analyses without identifying them as post hoc.
- A protocol, registration, or analysis plan specifies outcomes or analyses that are absent from the report.
- The reported findings appear to have been selected based on their direction, magnitude, or statistical significance.

If there is insufficient information to determine whether outcomes or analyses were selectively reported, select “Unclear” rather than assuming that reporting was complete.

# Criterion 7: Risk of Outcome Assessment Biases

This criterion is concerned with biases due to errors in applied statistical methods. It concerns statistical analysis, not the measurement or assessment of the outcome itself.

The checklist questions ask about four specific issues:

- Data analysts' awareness of the exposure or intervention received by subjects or areas.
- Errors in applied descriptive statistics, such as miscalculations of sample sizes, means, or variance.
- Errors in applied inferential statistics, including null hypothesis testing, estimation, and coding.
- Violation of assumptions for the applied inferential statistics and appropriateness of the applied statistical methods, such as criteria for normality and equal variances not being satisfied or inappropriate choice of statistical tests.

It is possible that all the other criteria are considered to have low risks of bias but there are errors in applied descriptive or inferential statistics.

If there is a substantive error in applied descriptive or inferential statistics, it is very likely that the outcome of a study, measure of effectiveness or impact, or inference will be changed. The algorithm therefore suggests high risk of bias when there is a substantive statistical error.

Minor typographical errors, rounding differences, or harmless reporting mistakes should not automatically be classified as high risk if they do not change the data, analysis, estimate, inference, or conclusion.

Failure to report an assumption check does not automatically demonstrate that an assumption was violated. If the appropriateness of the statistical method cannot be determined, select “Unclear” unless the available information provides evidence of a substantive violation or inappropriate analysis.

There may also be a case where no inferential statistics, such as hypothesis testing, is conducted in a study for specific findings of interest. For example, a meta-analysis may be interested in specific comparable raw data that are only a part of the study and for which there is no dedicated hypothesis testing. In such a case, assessors may select “Not applicable” for the relevant inferential-statistics checklist questions.

# Checklist Questions in Risk-of-Bias Criteria

The tool provides multiple checklist questions designed to help judgement about risk of bias within each risk-of-bias criterion.

Assessors are required to answer all checklist questions in the general category, as well as questions in the conditional category when the relevant conditions are met.

There is also an optional category for each risk-of-bias criterion. Optional questions allow assessors to communicate the magnitude and direction of potential bias or to consider results of quantitative assessment of risk of bias, such as through simulations.

If assessors feel that optional checklist questions are worth answering, they can answer them. Otherwise, they can skip them.

Detailed rationale or empirical evidence should be provided when predicting the magnitude and direction of bias.

The response options for the majority of checklist questions are:

- Yes (Y)
- Seemingly yes (SY)
- Seemingly no (SN)
- No (N)
- Unclear
- Not applicable (NA)

## Response-option definitions

- **Yes:** The paper explicitly states information confirming the answer.
- **No:** The paper explicitly states information contradicting the answer.
- **Seemingly yes:** The paper does not explicitly state the answer, but the available information strongly implies that the answer is yes.
- **Seemingly no:** The paper does not explicitly state the answer, but the available information strongly implies that the answer is no.
- **Unclear:** The question applies, but the paper does not provide enough information to determine whether the answer is yes or no.
- **Not applicable:** The question does not apply because the conditions required for the question are not met.

“Seemingly yes” and “seemingly no” should be used only when the available information supports a strong inference. If the evidence is insufficient to make a strong inference, select “Unclear.”

Assessors may select “Yes” or “No” when there is an explicit claim or information in available materials of a study.

When “Unclear” is selected, the default algorithm suggests a direction that favours higher risk of bias. “Unclear” does not mean that the study definitely has the relevant problem. It means that the available information does not establish whether the problem is present, and the default algorithm treats the uncertainty conservatively.

When conditions are not met, assessors can select “Not applicable.” “Not applicable” must not be used merely because the study does not report information. If a question applies but the study does not provide enough information, select “Unclear.”

Responses to all applicable checklist questions are needed for judging risk of bias in the individual risk-of-bias criteria and for judging the overall risk of bias for the study findings.

# Risk-of-Bias Judgement within Risk-of-Bias Criteria

Once assessors have responded to all checklist questions within a risk-of-bias criterion, they judge the risk of bias for that criterion.

The levels of risk of bias are:

- Low risk of bias (Low)
- Medium risk of bias (Med)
- High risk of bias (High)

The process of making a judgement about risk of bias follows the default decision rules for the criterion.

If an optional question has been answered through quantitative assessment, such as through simulation, the assessor's judgement about risk of bias may be upgraded or downgraded from the suggested judgement depending on the result of the quantitative assessment.

Detailed rationale or empirical evidence should be provided when predicting the magnitude and direction of bias or when changing the suggested judgement.

If the available information is insufficient to establish low risk of bias, the default algorithm may assign a higher risk category when an applicable response is “Unclear.” This is a conservative rule and does not mean that bias has been demonstrated.
